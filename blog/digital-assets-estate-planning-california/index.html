Your Executor Can't Just Log In: Digital Assets and Your California Estate Plan

A will names who gets your house. It doesn't get anyone into your email, your photo library, or your crypto wallet. California has a specific statute for that, and most estate plans still don't mention it.

Most people who come in for estate planning have thought hard about the house, the retirement accounts, maybe a piece of land in Paso Robles wine country. Almost nobody has thought about who gets into their email account, their cloud photo library, or the crypto wallet on their phone once they're gone. That gap used to be a minor inconvenience. Now it's routinely where an estate gets stuck — not because the law doesn't address it, but because the documents people signed years ago don't.

California does have a statute built for exactly this problem. It's called the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, and it's been on the books since 2016. Most clients have never heard of it, and most older wills and trusts were drafted before anyone thought to write the authorization the statute actually requires.

What Counts as a "Digital Asset" Under California Law

Probate Code sections 870 through 884 define a digital asset broadly: an electronic record in which a person has a right or interest. That covers cloud-stored photos, email, social media accounts, online banking and brokerage logins, domain names, digital subscriptions, and cryptocurrency. It does not cover the underlying financial value of an asset unless the asset is itself electronic — a digital record of a bank balance is a digital asset; the money in the account is not, because that's already governed by ordinary banking and probate rules.

The statute also draws a sharp line most people don't expect: it treats the content of your electronic communications very differently from the fact that they exist. Under Section 877, a custodian — Google, Apple, Meta, whoever holds the account — generally has to hand your personal representative a catalogue of your communications: who you emailed, and when, but not what you said. Getting the actual content of messages requires either your documented consent, typically through language in a will or trust drafted for this purpose, or a court order. A generic "I leave everything to my executor" clause doesn't do that job.

The Priority Order: Online Tool, Then Estate Plan, Then Terms of Service

California's law sets up a three-tier hierarchy for who controls access, and it matters because most people never engage with the first tier at all. If a platform offers a dedicated tool that lets you name a legacy contact or designate who gets access after death — separate from its ordinary terms of service — and you can change or revoke that choice at any time, that designation controls, full stop. It overrides your will, your trust, and your power of attorney. Google's Inactive Account Manager and Facebook's legacy contact setting both work this way.

If you never used one of those tools, your estate planning documents are the second tier: a will, trust, or power of attorney that expressly grants a fiduciary authority over digital assets will generally be honored by the custodian. If neither exists, the platform's own terms of service become the default rule, and most terms of service were written by companies trying to limit their own liability, not to help your family. That's the scenario RUFADAA was built to prevent, and it's still the scenario most people are in.

What Changed for Powers of Attorney in 2025

Until recently, California's version of the law worked cleanly for executors and trustees administering an estate after death, but was less clear about agents acting under a power of attorney while someone was still alive, or conservators appointed by the court. Senate Bill 1458 closed that gap, and it took effect January 1, 2025. It extended the same fiduciary-access framework to agents under a power of attorney and to court-appointed conservators, which matters far more than it might sound like. An aging parent who loses the ability to manage their own affairs still has bills on autopay, a bank that communicates by email, and accounts a caregiver or agent needs to reach — often before any question of a trust or probate ever comes up. If your power of attorney was drafted before 2025, or before digital assets were a standard consideration, it's worth having it reviewed against the current statute, particularly if you're also thinking ahead to elder law and conservatorship planning for yourself or a parent.

Custodians Still Have the Final Say on How Much They Hand Over

Even with clear legal authority, Section 875 gives the custodian discretion to grant either full account access or partial access limited to what the fiduciary actually needs to do their job — and the custodian can charge a reasonable fee for the work of producing it. In practice, this means a bank or a major tech company may hand over a spreadsheet of account activity rather than the keys to the account itself, even when your executor has every legal right to ask. That's a reasonable privacy safeguard, but it also means the paperwork matters. A fiduciary who shows up with a generic death certificate and no specific authorization language in the governing document is far more likely to get a partial response, or none, than one who has a will or trust that names digital assets explicitly.

What This Actually Means for Your Plan

Three things make the difference between an executor who can act and one who's stuck waiting on a subpoena:

  • Explicit authorization in your documents. A trust or will that specifically grants your fiduciary authority to access, manage, and close digital accounts — not just "all my property" — is what custodians are actually looking for under Section 875.
  • A written inventory, kept separate from your legal documents. A list of major accounts and where credentials are stored, updated periodically, saves your family from reconstructing your digital life from scratch. Don't put actual passwords in a will; wills often become public record in probate.
  • Someone who actually knows how to execute it. The person named to handle digital assets doesn't need to be your executor, but they need to be someone who can navigate a password manager, a crypto wallet, or a two-factor authentication reset without your help.

Cryptocurrency deserves a specific note: RUFADAA gives your fiduciary legal authority to request access, but it doesn't hand anyone your private keys or seed phrase. A wallet without recovery information isn't a legal problem — it's an unrecoverable asset. No court order fixes that. If you hold crypto, the seed phrase needs its own succession plan, stored securely and separately from your other documents, with instructions your fiduciary can actually follow.

None of this requires starting from scratch if you already have a trust or will. It usually means one focused amendment and a conversation about where your digital life actually lives. Frequently Asked Questions

If I name my spouse as executor in my will, does that automatically give them access to my email and social media?+
Not automatically, and not fully. A general executor designation typically gets your fiduciary a catalogue of your communications — who and when, not content — unless your will or trust specifically authorizes broader access, or a court orders it. Custodians look for that specific language under Probate Code Section 875.
Can my executor get into my cryptocurrency wallet after I die?+
Only if they have the private keys or seed phrase. RUFADAA gives your fiduciary the legal right to request access to digital assets, but it can't recover a wallet that nobody has the credentials for. Crypto holdings need a separate, secure plan for passing along recovery information.
What changed with Senate Bill 1458?+
Effective January 1, 2025, SB 1458 extended California's digital assets law to agents acting under a power of attorney and to court-appointed conservators, not just executors and trustees. That matters for anyone managing a living person's affairs, including aging parents.
Does using Google's Inactive Account Manager or Facebook's legacy contact mean I don't need to update my estate plan?+
Those tools control access to that specific platform and take priority over your will or trust for that account, but they don't cover your other accounts, your devices, or assets like cryptocurrency. They're a useful piece of a plan, not a substitute for one.
