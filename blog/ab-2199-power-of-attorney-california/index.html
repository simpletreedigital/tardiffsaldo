California's Power of Attorney Form Is Changing for the First Time Since 2011

AB 2199 rewrites the statutory form power of attorney to add successor agents, a built-in conservator nomination, and digital asset authority. It takes effect January 1, 2027, and it doesn't touch the documents you've already signed — which is exactly why it's worth knowing what's different before you assume yours is current.

California's statutory short form power of attorney, the one found at Probate Code section 4401, hasn't had a substantive rewrite since 2011. A lot has changed in fifteen years: people now hold significant assets in email accounts, cloud storage, and social media profiles that the form never contemplated, and the form offered no clean way to name a backup agent if your first choice couldn't serve. Assembly Bill 2199 fixes both gaps. It was signed into law this year, chaptered as Chapter 47 of the Statutes of 2026, and it takes effect January 1, 2027.

If you executed a power of attorney any time in the last decade and haven't looked at it since, this is a reasonable moment to pull it out and see whether it still does what you need it to do.

What AB 2199 Actually Changes

The bill repeals and re-enacts Probate Code section 4401, the statutory form itself, and adds a new section 4466. It does not touch the rules for who can sign, witness, or notarize a power of attorney — execution requirements stay the same. What changes is what the form lets you authorize. Three additions matter most:

  • Successor agents. The new form lets you name one or more backup agents who step in if your first agent dies, becomes incapacitated, resigns, or is otherwise unable or unwilling to serve.
  • Conservator nomination. You can now nominate your named agent, or a co-agent, to serve as conservator of your estate within the same statutory form, if a conservatorship ever becomes necessary.
  • Digital asset authority. The form adds express options tied to California's Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, letting you choose whether your agent gets access to the catalogue of your electronic communications, the content of those communications, or both.

Why the Successor Agent Option Is the Bigger Deal

Under the current form, if you named your spouse as agent and your spouse later predeceases you or develops dementia before you do, your power of attorney effectively goes dark. There's no backup built into the statutory form itself. Families in that situation have had two options: go back and execute a new document, or end up in a conservatorship proceeding because no one has authority to act. Neither is what anyone wants to be dealing with while also managing a health crisis.

AB 2199 closes that gap by letting you name a line of succession in the same document you'd already be signing. It's a small drafting change with an outsized practical effect, particularly for older clients whose first-choice agent is often a spouse close to them in age.

The Conservator Nomination Is New Ground

Before this bill, nominating who should serve as your conservator, if it ever came to that, usually happened in a separate document or was addressed informally in a will. Folding that nomination into the power of attorney itself means the person you've already trusted enough to manage your finances is the same person a court will look to first if a conservatorship petition is ever filed. California courts generally give weight to a validly executed nomination, which can shorten a conservatorship proceeding and reduce the chance that a judge appoints someone you wouldn't have chosen.

Digital Assets Catch Up With the Form

The three-tier digital asset option — catalogue only, content only, or both — mirrors the framework California adopted back in 2016 for digital executors, but the statutory power of attorney form never caught up to it until now. If you want a deeper look at what "catalogue" versus "content" actually means under California's digital assets law and how it plays out with specific platforms, we covered that in detail in our earlier piece on digital assets and your California estate plan. The short version for purposes of AB 2199: without an express grant, your agent may be limited to basic account information and locked out of actual message content, which matters more than people expect when someone else needs to manage your affairs.

Does This Affect the Power of Attorney You Already Signed?

No. AB 2199 changes the statutory form going forward; it doesn't retroactively amend documents already executed. A power of attorney you signed last year, or ten years ago, remains legally valid. It simply won't include the successor agent, conservator nomination, or expanded digital asset language unless you execute a new one.

What to Do Before January 1

You don't need to rush to re-sign anything the moment the new form takes effect. But it's a good prompt to review three things: whether your named agent is still the right choice and still able to serve, whether you'd want a successor named if something happened to that person, and whether your financial and digital life has changed enough that your existing document no longer covers what it should. For most people, a power of attorney is one piece of a broader estate plan built around a living trust and will, and it's worth reviewing alongside the rest of that plan rather than in isolation. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to redo my power of attorney before January 1, 2027?+
No. Your existing power of attorney remains valid. AB 2199 only changes what the statutory form offers going forward. You'd only need a new document if you want to add a successor agent, a conservator nomination, or expanded digital asset authority that your current one doesn't include.
Does AB 2199 change who can witness or notarize a power of attorney?+
No. The bill rewrites the statutory form itself and adds new sections on successor agents, conservator nomination, and digital assets. It does not change the existing signing, witnessing, or notarization requirements.
What happens if my named agent becomes unavailable and I never named a successor?+
Your power of attorney effectively stops working for that purpose, and your family may need to petition for a conservatorship to get someone legal authority to act on your behalf. That's a court process, and it's slower and more public than simply having named a backup agent in advance.
Can I use a custom power of attorney instead of the statutory form and still get these options?+
Yes. The statutory form in Probate Code section 4401 is optional, not mandatory. An attorney-drafted power of attorney can already include successor agents, conservator nominations, and digital asset provisions tailored to your situation. AB 2199 primarily affects people who use, or plan to use, the standardized statutory form.
