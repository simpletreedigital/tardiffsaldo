AB 251: When a Nursing Home Destroys Records, California Lowers the Bar of Proof

Elder abuse cases against care facilities have always been hard to win because the facility controls the evidence. A new law effective this year changes what happens when that evidence goes missing on purpose.

Elder abuse claims against residential care facilities and skilled nursing homes run into the same wall almost every time: the facility holds the charting notes, the staffing logs, the incident reports, and the video, and the family holds nothing but a bruise, a fall, or a death they can't fully explain. When those records are incomplete, or simply gone, proving abuse or neglect to the demanding "clear and convincing evidence" standard the law has traditionally required becomes close to impossible — even when everyone involved suspects the records disappeared for a reason.

Assembly Bill 251, authored by Assemblymember Ash Kalra and signed by the Governor on October 7, 2025, addresses that specific problem. It took effect January 1, 2026, and it's now been the law for most of this year, though it hasn't gotten nearly the attention that AB 2016's probate shortcut got earlier. Here's what it actually changed, and who it helps.

What AB 251 Actually Does

AB 251 amended Welfare and Institutions Code Section 15657 and added a new Section 15657.02 to the Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act (EADACPA). The change is narrow but significant: when a court finds that a residential care facility for the elderly or a skilled nursing facility engaged in spoliation of evidence, the court may apply a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard — the ordinary civil standard, meaning "more likely than not" — to the abuse, neglect, or abandonment claim, instead of requiring the family to meet the higher clear-and-convincing standard that EADACPA's enhanced remedies normally demand.

The statute defines spoliation of evidence as the intentional improper alteration of evidence, or the intentional concealment or destruction of records, documents, or other evidence, done with the intent to prevent that evidence from being produced, where the loss has materially prejudiced the other side's ability to prove its case. That's a deliberate, fairly high bar on its own — a facility that loses a file through ordinary sloppiness isn't automatically exposed to this rule. What the law targets is intentional destruction or concealment, not disorganization.

Why This Mattered Before AB 251

Under Section 15657, a plaintiff seeking EADACPA's enhanced remedies — attorney's fees and costs, recovery of the decedent's pre-death pain and suffering, and punitive damages — has to prove by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant acted with recklessness, oppression, fraud, or malice. That's a meaningfully higher bar than the preponderance standard used in most civil cases, and it exists for a reason: enhanced remedies are a serious consequence, and the legislature wanted a serious showing before they're available.

The problem families and their attorneys kept running into is that skilled nursing facilities and RCFEs are required by state and federal regulation to keep detailed records — care plans, medication logs, incident reports, staffing ratios. When a facility fails to produce those records, or when it turns out records were altered or destroyed after an incident, the family is left trying to clear a high evidentiary bar using exactly the evidence the facility made disappear. Before AB 251, a facility that destroyed incriminating records had, in effect, made its own case harder to prove against it, with no real legal consequence for having done so.

What Counts as Spoliation, and What Doesn't

The statute is specific about intent and prejudice. To trigger the preponderance standard, a family generally has to show:

  • The evidence was altered, concealed, or destroyed intentionally — not lost through routine record-retention gaps or an isolated clerical error
  • The facility acted with the purpose of keeping that evidence from being produced in litigation or an investigation, not for some unrelated administrative reason
  • The loss materially prejudiced the family's ability to prove the claim — meaning the missing evidence actually mattered to the case, not just that something is missing

A facility that can show a record was lost in an ordinary system migration, or destroyed under a routine document-retention policy that predates any claim, is in a different position than one that deletes camera footage the week after a family requests it. The statute is built to reach the second scenario, not the first.

Who This Helps, and Who It Doesn't

AB 251 is aimed specifically at claims against RCFEs and skilled nursing facilities — the licensed, regulated care settings where record-keeping duties are already spelled out by law. It doesn't change anything about financial elder abuse claims against a family member, caregiver, or acquaintance, which are governed by different sections of the same statute and don't typically turn on facility record-keeping in the same way. If you're dealing with a caregiver who drained a parent's bank account rather than a nursing home that mishandled a fall, this particular law isn't the one that applies to your situation — though EADACPA's fee-shifting and double-damages provisions still are.

It also doesn't lower the bar for proving the underlying abuse or neglect happened in the first place. Preponderance of the evidence is still a standard the family has to meet with real proof — it's simply a lower bar than clear and convincing, and one that becomes available only after a court has separately found that spoliation occurred. Getting there usually requires early, aggressive evidence preservation: a litigation hold letter sent promptly after an incident, a request for video before it's overwritten on a standard retention cycle, and a records subpoena that spells out exactly what's being demanded.

Why Speed Matters More Than Ever

Security camera footage at care facilities is often overwritten automatically within 30 to 90 days unless someone requests it be preserved. If a family waits months to consult an attorney after a fall, an unexplained injury, or a death, footage and other time-limited records can be gone before anyone asks for them — and a routine overwrite is a much harder thing to call "spoliation" than a deletion that happens right after a preservation request goes ignored.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Say a family's parent is a resident at a skilled nursing facility and suffers a serious fall that isn't reported to the family until days later. The facility's incident log for that shift is incomplete, and when the family's attorney sends a preservation letter requesting video and staffing records, the facility responds that the footage was "not retained." Before AB 251, the family's attorney would have to build a clear-and-convincing case for enhanced remedies without that footage. Now, if the family can show the facility knew about the incident, had the ability to preserve the footage, and let it lapse anyway after being asked to hold it, that pattern is exactly what Section 15657.02 was written to address — and it can shift the standard of proof back toward the family's favor rather than the facility's.

If You Suspect Neglect or Abuse at a Care Facility

The single most useful thing a family can do after a concerning incident at a nursing home or residential care facility is act quickly, before records rotate out on their normal retention schedule. That means requesting the resident's full chart, sending a written request that the facility preserve video and staffing records, and documenting what you observed and when, in your own words, while it's fresh. Frequently Asked Questions

Does AB 251 apply to abuse by a family member or private caregiver?+
No. AB 251 is specific to claims against residential care facilities for the elderly and skilled nursing facilities. Financial or physical abuse by a family member, private caregiver, or acquaintance is still governed by EADACPA's existing provisions, including the fee-shifting rule under Welfare and Institutions Code Section 15657.5, but the preponderance-of-evidence spoliation rule under Section 15657.02 doesn't extend to those cases.
Does losing a record automatically count as spoliation under the new law?+
No. The statute requires intentional alteration, concealment, or destruction done with the purpose of keeping the evidence from being produced, plus material prejudice to the other side's case. A record lost through routine retention schedules or an isolated clerical error generally would not meet that standard on its own.
When did AB 251 take effect?+
Governor Newsom signed AB 251 on October 7, 2025, and it took effect January 1, 2026, along with the rest of that year's legislative session.
What should I do if I think a facility destroyed evidence after my family member's fall or injury?+
Contact an attorney as soon as possible so a preservation letter can go out before video and other time-limited records are overwritten. Document what you already know in writing, and keep any communications with facility staff about the incident. The facility's response to a preservation request — whether it complies or claims the records are unavailable — can itself become part of the evidence.
