California's New $750,000 Probate Shortcut: What AB 2016 Means for San Luis Obispo County Homeowners

A new California law lets some families skip full probate on a primary residence worth up to $750,000. Given what homes actually cost in SLO County, that threshold solves the problem for fewer families here than the headlines suggest.

If you've read anything about California's new probate shortcut for homes under $750,000, you may have come away thinking your family's SLO County house is covered. For a meaningful share of homeowners here, it isn't. Assembly Bill 2016 amended Probate Code sections 13150 through 13154, effective April 1, 2025, and it genuinely helps some families avoid a full probate proceeding. But the $750,000 figure is a gross value cap on the home alone, and a lot of San Luis Obispo County real estate clears that number without much trouble.

Here's what the law actually does, who it helps, and why it's not a substitute for a properly funded trust if your property is worth what most property here is worth.

What AB 2016 Actually Changed

Before this law, California's simplified procedure for transferring a decedent's real property without full probate — the Petition to Determine Succession to Real Property under Probate Code Section 13151 — was tied to the same $184,500 small estate threshold that applies to personal property like bank accounts and vehicles. Because that threshold hadn't kept pace with California home values, almost no house in the state qualified, and nearly every family with a home in the decedent's name alone ended up in full probate regardless of how modest the estate otherwise was.

AB 2016 carves the decedent's primary residence out of that shared threshold and gives it its own, much higher cap: $750,000 in gross value, adjusted periodically by the Judicial Council. The personal property threshold stays at $184,500 and is unaffected. If the primary residence qualifies, the successor can file the simplified petition instead of opening a full probate case, which typically means months instead of a year or more, and meaningfully lower attorney and court costs.

Why $750,000 Doesn't Cover Most of SLO County

Gross value means the fair market value of the property, not the equity the family actually holds. A house with a $700,000 mortgage balance and a $750,000 market value is still valued at $750,000 for this purpose — the mortgage doesn't reduce the number that matters. And a modest home near downtown San Luis Obispo, in the Broad Street corridor, or close to Cal Poly now routinely clears $900,000 to $1.1 million. Coastal properties in Morro Bay, Avila Beach, and Shell Beach frequently sell well above that. For a large share of families in this county, the home alone exceeds the new threshold before you even look at the rest of the estate.

That's the gap that catches people off guard. AB 2016 got real, favorable press coverage when it passed, and it's easy to read a headline about a "$750,000 probate shortcut" and assume your house is covered. The number is real. Whether it applies to your specific property is a separate question, and it depends entirely on what the home is actually worth today, not what you paid for it or what the county assessor has it valued at for property tax purposes under Proposition 13.

How the Simplified Petition Works When It Does Apply

When a primary residence does fall at or under $750,000 in gross value, the successor — typically a surviving spouse, domestic partner, or the person named to inherit the property — files a Petition to Determine Succession to Real Property with the probate court in the county where the decedent lived. The petition requires an Inventory and Appraisal of the property, notice to heirs and beneficiaries, and a court hearing, but it skips the extended administration process, the requirement for a probate referee on every asset, and the multiple rounds of notice and accounting that a full probate case involves. Filed and handled correctly, it can resolve in a matter of months rather than the twelve to eighteen months typical of a contested or even routine full probate matter in SLO County Superior Court.

It still requires a court filing, a hearing date, and a valid legal basis for the successor's claim to the property. It is not a do-it-yourself form you fill out at the county recorder's office. Errors in the petition, disputes among heirs, or an appraisal that comes in over $750,000 after the petition is filed can all derail the simplified process and push the matter into full probate anyway.

What the Law Doesn't Fix

AB 2016 is a narrower fix than it sounds. It applies only to the decedent's primary residence, only up to the current gross value cap, and only when there's a clear successor with a valid claim. It does nothing for:

  • Second homes and rental property. A vacation property in Paso Robles wine country or a rental duplex in Atascadero still falls under the general $184,500 threshold if it's not the decedent's primary residence, which almost no investment property in this county meets.
  • Estates with disputes among heirs. The simplified petition assumes a straightforward successor. Contested claims, blended-family disagreements, or unclear wills still require full probate litigation.
  • Properties near or above the cap. If your home is worth $750,000 today, it may well be worth more by the time a probate matter is actually filed, especially given how SLO County property values have moved. Relying on a threshold you're close to is a risk, not a plan.
  • Anything beyond the house itself. Bank accounts, vehicles, business interests, and other individually titled assets are governed by separate thresholds and rules entirely.

The Actual Fix Is Still a Funded Trust

A properly funded revocable living trust avoids probate on the residence entirely, regardless of its value, and it avoids the court filing, the hearing, the appraisal requirement, and the public record that comes with any probate petition, simplified or not. AB 2016 is a meaningful improvement for families who end up in probate without a trust in place. It is not a reason to skip planning if your property is worth what SLO County property is generally worth. Frequently Asked Questions

Does AB 2016 apply automatically, or do I still have to file something?+
You still have to file. AB 2016 changes the threshold and creates a simplified petition process, but a successor must still file a Petition to Determine Succession to Real Property with the probate court, provide an Inventory and Appraisal, give notice to heirs, and appear at a hearing. Nothing transfers automatically.
Is the $750,000 threshold based on the county assessor's value or the market value?+
Market value. The petition requires a formal Inventory and Appraisal establishing the property's fair market value at the time of the filing, not the Prop 13 assessed value used for property tax purposes, which is typically much lower for long-held homes.
What happens if the appraisal comes back over $750,000 after I've already started the petition?+
The property no longer qualifies for the simplified procedure, and the matter typically has to proceed as a full probate case. This is one of the risks of relying on the shortcut for a property that's close to the threshold rather than clearly under it.
Does a living trust make AB 2016 irrelevant for my family?+
If your home and other major assets are properly titled in the name of a funded trust, neither AB 2016's shortcut nor full probate applies to those assets at all, because trust assets pass outside the probate process entirely. AB 2016 only matters for property still titled in an individual's name at death.
