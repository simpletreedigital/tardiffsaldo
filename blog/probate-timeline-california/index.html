What California Probate Actually Looks Like, From Petition to Final Distribution

Clients ask us some version of "how long is this going to take" within the first five minutes of almost every probate consultation. The honest answer depends on a series of fixed statutory deadlines that stack on top of each other — here's what they are and why the process takes as long as it does.

Most people who end up filing a probate petition have never done it before and won't do it again. They've usually heard that probate is slow and expensive and that a trust would have avoided it — all generally true — but that doesn't explain what's actually happening during the months the case sits open. It isn't sitting on a judge's desk the whole time. It's working through a sequence of notice periods and filing deadlines that California law builds into the process on purpose, mostly to protect creditors and heirs who haven't been found yet.

Here's that sequence, with the Probate Code sections behind each step, so you know what's a fixed deadline and what's just how long your particular court happens to be running.

Filing the Petition and Getting Notice Out

The process starts with a Petition for Probate (Judicial Council Form DE-111), filed with the superior court in the county where the decedent lived — for most of our clients, that's San Luis Obispo County Superior Court. Filing the petition doesn't give anyone authority to act yet. It just gets a hearing date on calendar, and most courts can't get one scheduled for several weeks.

Before that hearing happens, the petitioner has to give notice two separate ways. Under Probate Code Section 8110, the Notice of Petition to Administer Estate (Form DE-121) must be mailed to every heir and beneficiary at least 15 days before the hearing. Separately, Section 8121 requires the same notice to run in a newspaper of general circulation at least three times, with the first publication at least 15 days before the hearing and at least five days between the first and last run. Both exist so anyone with a legal interest — including heirs the petitioner may not know about — has a real chance to object before a representative is appointed.

Letters Issue, and Independent Administration Usually Kicks In

If nobody contests the petition at the hearing, the judge appoints a personal representative and the clerk issues Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration (Form DE-150). This is the moment that actually matters: the representative's legal authority, and the statutory clocks described below, both start running from the date letters are first issued, not the date of death and not the filing date.

Most petitions also request authority under the Independent Administration of Estates Act, Probate Code Sections 10400 through 10406. Full IAEA authority lets a representative handle most estate business — selling personal property, paying valid debts, even selling real property — without a judge's sign-off on each transaction, which is the biggest single thing keeping probate from requiring a dozen separate hearings. The tradeoff is procedural, not a free pass: before taking an action listed in Sections 10550 through 10564, the representative must send a Notice of Proposed Action (Form DE-165) to every heir, beneficiary, and affected creditor at least 15 days in advance, under Section 10580. Recipients then have 15 days to object under Section 10587. No objection, and the action proceeds without the court ever seeing it; an objection sends that transaction back to a hearing.

The Four-Month Clock: Creditors and the Inventory

Two separate four-month deadlines start on the date letters are issued, and they run in parallel, not in sequence.

First, Probate Code Section 8800 requires the representative to file an Inventory and Appraisal listing everything in the estate, within four months of letters issuing. Cash accounts can usually be valued directly, but real property, vehicles, and other non-cash assets have to be appraised by a probate referee — appointed by the state controller's office to a particular county, SLO County included, specifically to value estate assets.

Second, Probate Code Section 9100 sets the deadline for creditors to file claims: the later of four months after letters are first issued, or 60 days after the representative mails that creditor individual notice. A creditor who misses both deadlines is generally barred from collecting through the probate case at all. There's an absolute outer limit regardless of how the case is going: under Code of Civil Procedure Section 366.2, no creditor can bring a claim against a decedent's estate more than one year after the date of death, period.

What the Timeline Actually Adds Up To

Run those deadlines together and you get the real answer to "how long does this take." The four-month creditor claim period alone sets a floor of roughly six months for even the simplest, uncontested estate, once you add the weeks needed to get the initial hearing on calendar and to prepare a final accounting afterward. A straightforward estate with a cooperative sole heir and no property disputes typically closes in nine to twelve months. Add a house that needs to be sold, multiple beneficiaries, or any disagreement over who should serve as representative, and twelve to eighteen months is common even without anyone formally contesting anything. A genuinely contested estate — a will contest, a dispute over an heir's status, a fight over the representative's conduct — can run two years or longer, since each dispute typically requires its own noticed motion and hearing.

What Probate Actually Costs

Probate Code Section 10810 sets statutory compensation for both the personal representative and the estate's attorney, calculated separately on the same percentage schedule: 4% of the first $100,000 of the estate's gross value, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, 1% of the next $9,000,000, and smaller percentages above that. The number that catches people off guard is "gross value." A home worth $950,000 with a $700,000 mortgage still counts as $950,000 for this calculation — the fee is based on what the asset is worth, not the family's actual equity. On a $950,000 gross estate, that schedule works out to roughly $25,500 in statutory attorney fees, and the representative is entitled to request the identical amount, before court costs, publication fees, or the probate referee's fee are added.

That's the practical argument for a funded revocable trust, which our successor trustee administration covers in more detail: trust assets never go through this process or this fee schedule at all. It's also why California created a narrower alternative for smaller estates, which we've written about separately in our piece on AB 2016's $750,000 probate shortcut for primary residences.

Closing the Estate

Once the creditor claim period has run and the inventory is on file, the representative petitions the court for an Order for Final Distribution, typically supported by an accounting showing every receipt, disbursement, and remaining asset since letters issued — unless every beneficiary has waived a formal accounting in writing, which is common when the beneficiaries are getting along. The court sets a hearing, signs the order, and the representative distributes what's left under the will or the laws of intestate succession. For an uncontested estate administered under independent authority, this final hearing is often only the second time anyone appears in front of a judge in the entire case.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does probate actually take in California?+
The mandatory four-month creditor claim period alone creates a floor of roughly six months for even the simplest estate. Most straightforward, uncontested estates close in nine to twelve months. Twelve to eighteen months is common once you add a house sale or multiple beneficiaries, and contested estates can run two years or more.
Can the personal representative sell estate property without going back to court every time?+
Usually, yes, if the petition requested authority under the Independent Administration of Estates Act. The representative still has to send a Notice of Proposed Action to every heir, beneficiary, and affected creditor at least 15 days before the sale or other listed action, and anyone who objects within that 15-day window can force the matter back in front of a judge.
What happens if a creditor doesn't file a claim in time?+
A claim filed after the deadline under Probate Code Section 9100 — four months after letters issue, or 60 days after that creditor received individual notice, whichever is later — is generally barred. Regardless of how the probate case itself is proceeding, no creditor can bring a claim against the estate more than one year after the date of death under Code of Civil Procedure Section 366.2.
How much does probate actually cost?+
The personal representative and the estate's attorney are each separately entitled to statutory fees under Probate Code Section 10810, calculated on the estate's gross value using the same percentage schedule: 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, and declining percentages above that. Gross value means debts and mortgages are not subtracted first, so the fee is often larger than families expect relative to the estate's actual equity.
