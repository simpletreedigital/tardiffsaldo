Nuclear Verdicts Are Rising Nationally. Here's What That Has to Do With Your Injury Claim.

Jury verdicts of $10 million or more hit a record high in 2025. Almost nobody reading this will ever see a courtroom, let alone a jury — but that trend is already changing how insurance adjusters evaluate ordinary claims, including yours.

If you've filed a personal injury claim recently and the adjuster's first offer felt insultingly low, or the insurer suddenly wanted an independent medical exam for an injury that used to get resolved with a demand letter and a few phone calls, you're not imagining a pattern. Insurance companies have been watching the same data everyone else in the industry is watching, and they've adjusted how they handle claims across the board — not just the handful that actually go to trial.

What "Nuclear Verdicts" Actually Means

The research and communications firm Marathon Strategies has tracked jury verdicts of $10 million or more against corporate defendants since 2023, publishing an annual "Nuclear Verdicts" report. The 2024 figures, released in 2025, counted 135 such verdicts — a 52% jump over 2023 and the highest annual total the firm had identified in years. The report issued in August 2026, covering 2025, found the number climbed again: nearly 200 verdicts of $10 million or more, roughly a 41% increase over 2024. More than 40 of those verdicts topped $100 million, and four exceeded $1 billion.

This isn't limited to one industry's research. The Swiss Re Institute's Social Inflation Index, which measures how fast liability claim costs are rising beyond ordinary inflation, hit 7% in 2023, its highest level in two decades. And a RAND Corporation study of civil trial outcomes found that the share of personal injury and wrongful death cases ending in a verdict for the plaintiff rose from 53% in 2010 to 64% in 2019, while inflation-adjusted trial awards in those case types grew at a compound rate of about 7.6% a year over the same period.

Why a Few Dozen Huge Verdicts Change How Your Ordinary Claim Gets Handled

Insurance companies price risk across their entire book of claims, not case by case. When the tail end of that distribution — the rare catastrophic verdict — gets bigger and more frequent, actuaries revise what the whole pool is expected to cost, and that revision shows up long before any individual case gets near a jury. Insurers have responded, by their own account in industry filings and trade press, by pushing harder on claims that would once have settled routinely: more disputes over whether an injury is causally connected to the incident, more independent medical exams, more use of outside defense counsel earlier in the process, and lower initial offers aimed at claimants who haven't yet talked to an attorney.

None of that means your case is suddenly worth millions because some unrelated verdict somewhere made headlines. It means the person on the other side of your claim has been told, as a matter of underwriting policy, to resolve more disputes in-house and resist more claims that used to get paid without much friction. The practical effect for an injured person is the same either way: a wider gap between what a claim is actually worth and what the first offer looks like.

What This Looks Like From the Claimant's Side

In practice, the shift tends to show up in a few predictable ways. Initial settlement offers arrive faster and lower, often before medical treatment is even finished, timed to reach claimants before they've retained a lawyer. Soft-tissue and whiplash-type injuries, long treated by adjusters as routine, get more aggressive causation challenges. Recorded statements get requested earlier, and more claims that used to resolve with a demand letter now require a filed lawsuit before the insurer will negotiate seriously. None of these tactics are new on their own. What's changed is how consistently they're being used on claims that, five years ago, would have settled at the first or second round of negotiation.

What To Do Differently

  • Don't sign anything, or give a recorded statement, before you understand what you're agreeing to. An early medical authorization or a recorded statement given without counsel can be used to narrow your claim later, even if nothing you said was false.
  • Finish your medical treatment before you evaluate any settlement number. An offer made while you're still being treated is, by definition, based on incomplete information about what your injury actually costs you.
  • Know California's comparative negligence rule before you accept a low number tied to shared fault. California follows a pure comparative negligence rule, established by the California Supreme Court in Li v. Yellow Cab Co. (1975), which lets an injured person recover damages even if they were partly at fault, reduced by their own percentage of responsibility. Adjusters sometimes present shared fault as if it eliminates a claim; under California law, it only reduces it.
  • Bring in an attorney before the insurer's position hardens, not after. If claims are being resolved more often through litigation than through early negotiation, the point at which legal representation helps has moved earlier, not later.

This pattern plays out the same way whether the injury happened on Highway 101, in a parking lot, or anywhere else in the county. Frequently Asked Questions

Does the rise in nuclear verdicts mean my case is worth more money?+
Not directly. Nuclear verdicts are rare, extreme outcomes in cases that actually go to trial, usually against large corporate defendants. What they change is how insurers underwrite and handle claims broadly, which affects the negotiating posture on your claim. The value of your specific case still depends on your medical evidence, the liability facts, and your documented damages.
Is California seeing more of these large verdicts than other states?+
Marathon Strategies' reports break results down by state, and other states have led the rankings in recent years. The underlying trend toward tougher claims handling, however, shows up across jurisdictions, including California, because it reflects how insurers set national claims policy rather than a single state's court outcomes.
Should I accept a quick settlement offer from an insurance adjuster?+
Be cautious of any offer made before your medical treatment is complete or before you've spoken with an attorney. Once you sign a release, you typically can't go back and ask for more if your injury turns out to be worse or more expensive than it looked at first.
What is California's comparative negligence rule?+
Under the pure comparative negligence rule established in Li v. Yellow Cab Co. (1975), an injured person in California can still recover damages even if they were partly at fault for the incident. Their recovery is reduced by their own percentage of fault, but it isn't eliminated, even if that percentage is high.
