What Happens After You Report a Dog Bite in San Luis Obispo County

Calling SLO County Animal Services starts a process that runs on its own track, separate from your injury claim: a quarantine, sometimes a dangerous-dog hearing, and a paper trail that can end up mattering more than people expect. Here's how each piece works and what it does, and doesn't, do for the money side of your case.

Most people who call us after a dog bite have already done the right things instinctively: they got treated, they got the owner's information, and somewhere in there they called animal control, or someone else did it for them. What they usually haven't been told is what happens on the other end of that call, and why it's worth understanding even though it runs almost entirely separate from the civil claim that will actually pay your medical bills.

The Report Triggers a Quarantine, Not a Liability Finding

Once a bite is reported to SLO County Animal Services at (805) 781-4400, the department opens a rabies quarantine on the dog, typically for ten days. The purpose is narrow: confirm the dog isn't carrying rabies and wasn't showing signs of it at the time of the bite. It has nothing to do with whether the owner is liable to you. Under California Civil Code section 3342, that question is already settled the moment the bite happens, as long as you were in a public place or lawfully on the property where it occurred. The owner doesn't get to wait and see how the quarantine comes back before being on the hook.

People sometimes assume a clean quarantine, no rabies, no signs of aggression noted, somehow helps the dog's owner or hurts their claim. It doesn't. Civil Code 3342 was written specifically so victims don't have to prove the dog had a history or that the owner was careless. The quarantine and the claim are two different processes answering two different questions.

When a Dog Can Be Declared Potentially Dangerous or Vicious

A single bite report doesn't automatically put a dog's future at risk. California's framework for that lives in Food and Agricultural Code sections 31621 and 31622, which set out a hearing process for declaring a dog "potentially dangerous" or "vicious," generally reserved for dogs with a pattern of unprovoked aggression or more than one bite incident. At that hearing, the county or a hearing officer it designates decides the question on a preponderance of the evidence, meaning more likely than not, and the dog's owner is allowed to present evidence against the designation. Afterward, the owner has to be notified in writing, personally or by first-class mail, and an unfavorable decision from a hearing officer can be appealed to the superior court.

Why San Luis Obispo County Has a Direct Stake in How This Works

This isn't an abstract statewide framework for us. The 1986 case that first established dog owners' due process rights in these hearings, Phillips v. San Luis Obispo County Department of Animal Regulation, came out of this county. The Phillips family's dog had bitten a child more than once, and the department moved to destroy the dog without the kind of hearing the family thought they were entitled to, offering what the court later called a "courtesy hearing" instead. The California Court of Appeal held that wasn't good enough: because a dog is personal property with real value to its owner, due process requires an actual hearing, with real notice, before the government can seize or destroy it, unless there's an emergency need to act first. That case still shapes how these hearings are run today, not just here but statewide.

Does the Dangerous-Dog Process Affect My Settlement?

Not directly, and not quickly. A dangerous-dog hearing can take weeks to schedule and resolve, while most insurance claims move on their own timeline once your treatment stabilizes. Where the two intersect is evidence: if animal control's file documents a prior bite or a pattern of aggression, that record can undercut an insurer's argument that the attack was a one-time fluke, even though California law doesn't require that proof in the first place.

What the Animal Control File Actually Does for Your Claim

Because Civil Code 3342 doesn't require you to prove the dog had a history, you might wonder why the report matters at all for the money side of things. Two reasons. First, it creates a timestamped, third-party record of when and where the bite happened, which is useful if the insurer later tries to dispute the circumstances. Second, if the animal control file shows a prior incident, even an informal one that never went to a full hearing, it strengthens the case for punitive or aggravated damages and makes an insurer far less likely to argue the attack was unforeseeable. We request the full animal control file as a matter of course in every case we handle, as discussed in our earlier piece on how insurers evaluate injury claims, and it's often the difference between a quick, lowball offer and one that reflects what the case is actually worth.

What to Do If You're Still in the Reporting Window

If you were bitten recently and haven't reported it yet, do it regardless of how minor the injury looks. Wounds that seem superficial can become infected, and a report made within days of the bite is far more useful than one reconstructed from memory months later. SLO County Animal Services can be reached at (805) 781-4400; Santa Barbara County incidents go to that county's Animal Services at (805) 934-6456. Keep a copy of whatever report number or confirmation you receive, and don't let the quarantine or hearing process convince you to wait before talking to a lawyer about your own claim. The two tracks run in parallel, and nothing about the animal control process extends or shortens your own filing deadline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Am I legally required to report a dog bite in San Luis Obispo County?+
Yes. California law requires that animal bites be reported to local health or animal control authorities, which is what triggers the quarantine process. Beyond the legal requirement, reporting also creates an official, timestamped record that can matter later if your claim is disputed.
Does the quarantine period affect how long my claim takes?+
Not directly. The quarantine is a separate administrative process focused on rabies monitoring. Your injury claim proceeds on its own timeline, generally driven by how long your medical treatment takes to stabilize, not by the quarantine's ten-day window.
If the dog isn't declared dangerous, does that hurt my case?+
No. Under Civil Code section 3342, you don't need the dog to carry any formal dangerous-dog designation to hold the owner liable. California's strict liability rule applies to the first bite, with or without a hearing or prior history.
Can I request the animal control file on the dog that bit me?+
Generally yes, and we do this routinely as part of building a claim. The file can document prior incidents, the quarantine outcome, and any dangerous-dog proceedings, all of which can support a stronger claim even though they aren't legally required to establish liability.
What's the deadline to file a lawsuit over a dog bite in California?+
Two years from the date of the bite under Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1, regardless of how the animal control or quarantine process resolves. For bites involving a minor, the deadline doesn't start running until the child turns 18.
