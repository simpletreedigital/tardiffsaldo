Vineyard Succession Planning in Paso Robles: Why a Trust Alone Isn't Enough

A living trust will move your house to your kids without probate. It won't tell them who manages the vineyard, who can sell their share, or what happens to the land's tax base the day it changes hands. Wine country families need a second layer of planning that most estate plans skip.

Most of the estate planning conversations we have in Paso Robles start the same way a conversation anywhere else in SLO County starts: a family wants a trust so their house avoids probate. Then it comes out, usually a few minutes in, that there's also eleven acres of vineyard behind the house, a crush contract with a local winery, and two adult children who disagree about whether either of them wants to keep farming it. At that point, the house is the easy part.

A vineyard is a piece of real estate and a working business sitting on top of it, and those two things don't transfer the same way or face the same tax rules. Treating a wine country estate like a residential estate plan is where families in North County get into trouble.

Prop 19 Took Away the Break That Used to Cover Land Like This

Before February 2021, California parents could transfer up to $1 million of assessed value in real property other than their primary residence — a second home, a rental, a vineyard parcel — to their children without reassessment. Proposition 19 eliminated that exclusion outright. It wasn't narrowed; it's gone.

What's left is a single, limited exclusion that applies only to a parent's primary residence, and only if the child actually moves in and claims it as their own primary residence within a year. A working vineyard parcel that isn't anyone's home doesn't qualify for that exclusion at all. If your vineyard land sits on a Prop 13 assessed base from a purchase decades ago and the next generation inherits it outright, the county reassesses it to current market value the moment the transfer happens. In a wine country market where acreage with an established vineyard regularly trades well above the raw land value of comparable North County parcels, that reassessment is not a rounding error. It can permanently reset the property tax on land that's been in the family's assessed base for a generation.

None of this is a reason to panic, and it isn't a reason to rush a transfer before you understand it, either. It's a reason to run the actual numbers on your specific parcel's assessed value against its current value before deciding how, or when, the land changes hands. An LLC Protects the Business. It Doesn't Replace a Succession Plan.

Most working vineyards and small wineries in this region operate through an LLC or a family limited partnership rather than individual ownership, and for good reason: it separates the farming and winemaking liability from the family's personal assets, and it lets a trust hold a single membership interest instead of a tangle of equipment, contracts, and crop insurance policies. That's good structure. It is not, by itself, a succession plan.

An operating agreement that was drafted to form the entity and satisfy the bank usually says nothing about what happens when the founding generation is gone. Who has authority to make farming decisions during harvest? Can a member who wants out force a sale of the whole operation, or only a buyout of their own interest? Does the crush contract or the lease with a Westside winery survive a change in who holds the membership interest, or does it need to be assigned? These are the questions that matter the day an owner dies or becomes incapacitated, and an operating agreement silent on succession leaves siblings to work it out themselves, usually at the worst possible time. Our business law team drafts and reviews operating agreements and buy-sell provisions specifically to close that gap before it becomes a problem.

What Happens Without a Plan: Probate on a Working Operation

California's simplified small-estate procedures top out at $184,500 in gross asset value, and the gross value of a working vineyard, its equipment, its wine inventory in barrel or bottle, and the land itself clears that threshold almost immediately. Without a funded trust holding title to the land and the entity interest, the estate goes through full probate — and a probate court does not run a vineyard.

During a contested or even routine probate matter, decisions about irrigation, pruning schedules, harvest timing, and contract renewals either stall waiting on court approval or fall to whoever is willing to act without clear authority, which creates its own liability. We handle estates like this through our probate practice when families come to us after the fact, but the better outcome is never needing that process in the first place. A trust that actually holds the vineyard's real property and entity interest keeps the operation running under a successor trustee's authority the day after a death, with no court filing and no public proceeding.

Buy-Sell Agreements: The Document That Keeps Harvest Going When One Sibling Wants Out

The most common fracture point we see in multi-generational wine country families isn't disagreement about the land's value. It's that one sibling wants to keep farming and the other wants to be bought out in cash, and nobody wrote down in advance how that buyout gets priced, funded, or timed. Without an agreement, a sibling who wants out has real leverage to force a sale of the entire operation, which is rarely what either side actually wants.

A buy-sell agreement, drafted while everyone is still getting along, fixes a valuation method in advance, sets payment terms the business can actually absorb without emptying its operating accounts mid-harvest, and can be funded in part through life insurance so cash is available without selling land under pressure. It's one document, and it's the single highest-leverage piece of planning for any family business with more than one eventual owner.

If Your Land Is Under a Williamson Act Contract

Agricultural preserve contracts affect your property's assessed value separately from Prop 13 and Prop 19, and they can carry their own notice and continuation requirements when ownership changes. If any part of your vineyard is enrolled under a Williamson Act contract with the county, confirm how a transfer affects that contract's status before you restructure ownership or move land into an entity or trust.

Building a Plan That Actually Covers the Vineyard

A coordinated plan for a working wine country estate typically layers three things: a funded revocable trust holding the real property and any entity membership interest, so nothing lands in probate; an operating or partnership agreement with clear succession, management, and buyout provisions for the business itself; and a current picture of the Prop 19 exposure on the land so the family isn't surprised by a reassessment they didn't plan around. Each piece is manageable on its own. Skipping any one of them is usually what turns a transition into a dispute.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a living trust avoid Prop 19 reassessment on vineyard land?+
A trust avoids probate, but it does not create a property tax exclusion Prop 19 doesn't already provide. Moving the vineyard into your own revocable trust during your lifetime isn't a change of ownership for reassessment purposes, so it doesn't trigger reassessment either. The exposure comes later, when the property actually passes to the next generation, and a trust determines how smoothly that happens, not whether the county reassesses.
Can one sibling be forced to sell their share of the family vineyard?+
Without a buy-sell agreement or clear provisions in the operating agreement, a co-owner who wants out can, in some circumstances, petition a court to force a sale or division of jointly held property. A properly drafted buy-sell agreement replaces that uncertainty with a fixed, pre-agreed process for one owner to buy out another instead.
Does the vineyard's LLC need to be mentioned specifically in the trust?+
Yes. A trust only controls assets that have actually been transferred, or "funded," into it. If the membership interest in the vineyard's LLC was never formally assigned to the trust, that interest can end up in probate even though the trust document exists and even though the real estate was properly funded.
We're still deciding whether to form an LLC for the vineyard. Does that change the estate planning?+
It changes it significantly. Land held in individual names is titled directly into the trust. Land and equipment held through an entity are addressed through the trust holding the entity interest, plus an operating agreement governing the entity itself. Deciding the business structure and the estate plan at the same time, rather than sequentially, avoids redoing paperwork later.
