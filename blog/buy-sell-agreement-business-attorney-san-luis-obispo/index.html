Buy-Sell Agreements: What a Business Attorney in San Luis Obispo Wants Every Partner to Put in Writing

Most business partnerships start with a handshake and a template operating agreement pulled off the internet. That document usually says nothing about what happens when one partner dies, wants out, or stops showing up. Here is what a buy-sell agreement actually does about that, and why the clause partners most often ask us to add to one usually will not hold up in California.

As a business attorney in San Luis Obispo, we draft a lot of operating agreements and shareholder agreements, and we see the same gap in nearly all of them: the document covers day-to-day management, voting, and profit splits, but says little or nothing about what happens when an owner leaves the picture. Not "leaves" in the sense of quitting a job. Leaves because they died, got divorced, filed for bankruptcy, became disabled, or simply decided they were done. A buy-sell agreement is the piece of paper that answers that question before it becomes an emergency.

Any California business lawyer in San Luis Obispo who has handled a partner exit will tell you the same thing: the time to write this document is when everyone still likes each other, not after.

What a Buy-Sell Agreement Actually Covers

A buy-sell agreement is a contract between co-owners, or between the owners and the business entity itself, that sets out what happens to an owner's share when a defined triggering event occurs. The triggering events are usually the same handful across most agreements we draft: death, permanent disability, divorce, personal bankruptcy, a voluntary decision to leave, and an involuntary removal for cause. The agreement answers three practical questions for each of those events: who has the right or obligation to buy the departing owner's interest, how that interest gets valued, and how the purchase actually gets paid for.

These provisions can live inside an LLC operating agreement or a corporate shareholder agreement, or they can stand alone as a separate buy-sell contract that references the entity's governing documents. Either way, the business itself still has to be formed and maintained correctly for any of this to matter, which for most of our clients means an LLC or corporation registered with the California Secretary of State.

What Happens When There Isn't One

Without a buy-sell agreement, a deceased or departing owner's interest typically passes according to whatever default rule applies, which usually means it goes to their estate, their spouse, or their heirs. Those are often people who have no relationship with the business and no interest in running it, sitting on the other side of the table from the surviving partners with full voting and economic rights. The surviving owners are then negotiating a buyout from scratch, under pressure, with no agreed valuation method and no funding plan, often while also grieving a partner or dealing with the fallout of a divorce or a dispute.

We see this surface most often in two ways. First, a partner dies and the surviving owner discovers they are now in business with their late partner's spouse or adult children. Second, two partners disagree badly enough that one wants out, and neither the operating agreement nor anything else says what the buyout price should be or who decides it. Both situations are solvable, but they take far longer and cost far more to solve after the fact than they would have cost to prevent.

Why the Operating Agreement Alone Usually Isn't Enough

Most of the operating agreements and shareholder agreements we review were not written by a business lawyer in San Luis Obispo or anywhere else. They were adapted from a template, filed with the state, and never touched again. That document typically covers management structure, voting thresholds, and how profits get split. It rarely addresses ownership transfer in any real detail, and when it does, the language is often too general to apply cleanly to an actual death, divorce, or falling-out years later when the business looks nothing like it did at formation.

A buy-sell provision has to be specific enough to function without further negotiation: a stated valuation method (a formula, a fixed price revisited annually, or an appraisal process), a payment structure (lump sum, installment note, or life insurance proceeds for a death-triggered buyout), and a defined timeline for each step. Vague language that sounds reasonable in the abstract, like "fair market value as agreed by the parties," is exactly the kind of clause that produces a dispute instead of preventing one, because it assumes the parties will still be able to agree once the triggering event has already happened.

The Non-Compete Clause That Usually Doesn't Hold Up

Partners negotiating a buyout often want to add a non-compete: the departing owner agrees not to open a competing business nearby for some period of time. In California, that clause is risky to rely on. Business and Professions Code Section 16600 voids most non-compete agreements as against public policy, and the legislature strengthened that rule further with SB 699 and AB 1076, both effective in 2024, which made it unlawful for a business to even attempt to enforce a non-compete that does not fall within one of the statute's narrow exceptions.

There is an exception for a genuine sale of a business and its goodwill, but it is narrower than most partners expect, and recent California appellate decisions have scrutinized it closely rather than applying it loosely to any buyout that happens to include a non-compete clause. The practical lesson is not to skip the conversation about competition after a buyout, but to have it with a business attorney before the agreement is signed, so the departing partner's obligations are built around what California law will actually enforce, such as protecting trade secrets and client confidentiality, instead of a blanket non-compete that a court is likely to strike down anyway.

What This Actually Costs

A properly drafted buy-sell agreement is a modest expense compared to litigating an ownership dispute without one. The cost of drafting these provisions correctly is a small fraction of what a contested buyout or a forced accounting action runs once lawyers, appraisers, and court time are added up on both sides. Partners who put this off are usually not avoiding the cost. They are just moving it later, onto whichever version of themselves has to deal with it during an actual triggering event, and attaching a much larger number to it.

Business Succession Is Part of This, Not Separate From It

For family businesses and closely held companies, a buy-sell agreement overlaps directly with personal estate planning. If an owner's interest passes to their estate at death, how that interest is then distributed, and to whom, depends on their personal trust and estate plan, not just the business's governing documents. Frequently Asked Questions

Do we need a buy-sell agreement if our business only has two owners?+
Yes, and arguably a two-owner business needs one more than a larger one. With only two owners, there is no board or majority of remaining partners to absorb a sudden ownership change. If one owner dies or exits without an agreement in place, the other is left negotiating directly with that person's estate, spouse, or heirs, often while also trying to keep the business running.
What happens to my ownership interest if I die without a buy-sell agreement in place?+
Your interest typically passes according to your personal estate plan, or to your legal heirs if you do not have one, which can put your business partners into an ownership relationship with people who have no experience running the business and no desire to. A buy-sell agreement gives your partners a defined, funded way to buy out that interest instead.
Can a buy-sell agreement include a non-compete for the partner who leaves?+
It can try to, but California law makes most non-compete clauses unenforceable under Business and Professions Code Section 16600, a rule the legislature strengthened in 2024. A narrow exception exists for a genuine sale of a business and its goodwill, but it is applied carefully by California courts and should not be assumed to cover every buyout. Protecting trade secrets and client relationships through other means is usually a sturdier approach than relying on a non-compete.
How is the buyout price usually determined?+
The agreement itself sets the method, which is the whole point of having one. Common approaches include a fixed price the owners update annually, a formula based on revenue or profit, or an independent appraisal triggered by the departure event. Agreements that simply say the price will be "agreed upon" at the time tend to produce disputes rather than prevent them, since that is exactly when the parties are least likely to agree.
